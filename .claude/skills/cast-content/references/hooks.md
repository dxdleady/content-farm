# Hooks

Slide 1 decides whether the other eight are read. Nothing else in this system matters as
much: a weak hook on a beautiful deck is a deck nobody swipes.

This file is the working reference for writing one. It is meant to grow — when a hook
works, add it to the library at the bottom; when one fails in a new way, add the trap.

---

## The five templates

From other verticals, where they are proven. The mechanics transfer; the subject does not.
**They are examples, not a form to fill in** — write your own. What binds is the trait list
in the next section, not these shapes.

| # | Template | Original |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | How long it actually takes to `<get the result>` | How long it actually takes to see results |
| 2 | `<N>` ways to `<get the result>` | 5 ways to copy/paste your dream body onto you |
| 3 | Here is how you can actually `<get the result>` | Here is how you can actually reach your dream body |
| 4 | `<N>` ways to `<get the result>` **without** `<the part they dread>` | 5 ways to reach your goal body without lifting weights |
| 5 | Here is how you know `<the thing they cannot judge alone>` | Here is how you know how much you should workout to reach your dream body |

Note what all five share and what none of them do: every one promises the reader
information about **their own outcome**, and not one names a product, a feature or a brand.

---

## The traits — this is the actual rule

A hook with these is a good hook whatever shape it takes. A hook missing them fails
however closely it copies a template.

- **Second person.** "your edit", "you can" — not "an episode", not "one hour of tape".
- **A concrete deliverable, promised in the first six words.**
- **Plain, blunt English.** "How long it actually takes" — not "Why one hour of tape eats
  a whole evening". If it reads like a magazine subhead, it is the wrong register.
- **Names the thing they dread**, where there is one. The single strongest move available:
  state the outcome AND remove the part they have been avoiding, in the same sentence.
- **No product.** Not a brand, not a feature, not a company.

## A hook is a promise, and the deck must pay it

Promise a number and the deck holds exactly that many, named and countable. Promise a
duration and a slide states it. Promise a way to judge and a slide gives the test.

Writing a hook the body does not deliver is worse than a dull one: the reader swiped for
something specific and did not get it. This cuts both ways — it is also why you must not
promise a count you would have to invent.

**An idiom is not a statistic.** "An evening a week", "a whole weekend", "half your
Sunday" are colloquial units of effort and need no source — they are how people describe
time to each other. What needs sourcing is a figure presented as a MEASUREMENT: "3× faster",
"70% of podcasters", "saves 4 hours per episode". The test is whether a reader would expect
a citation behind it. Applying the sourcing rule to ordinary English stops you writing
anything, and flagging an idiom as an unsourced claim wastes the writer's turn.

**Do not reach for a number by default.** "N ways to…" is one option among five, and
picking it out of habit has a cost: the count then dictates the body. A deck bent into
five buckets it did not have produces five artificial, overlapping ones. Use a number when
the content genuinely holds that many countable things; otherwise use a shape that does
not promise a count.

## Moves — what the hook DOES to the reader

The five templates above are all one move: *promise information about your outcome*. It is
the safest and the right default, but it is not the only one. Below are the moves worth
having, each with the reason it works — **the psychology is the part that transfers**. A
template you can copy gets you one hook; knowing why it works lets you write the sixth.

### The moves a product account can actually use

| move | why it works | shape | for this product |
|---|---|---|---|
| **loss** | Loss aversion beats gain-seeking. Naming what they are *already* paying lands harder than promising a benefit. | "You're losing `<metric>` by `<ordinary habit>`" | "You're losing an evening a week to nine passes over the same hour" |
| **tiny change, big impact** | A small action feels achievable; a disproportionate result creates the gap. | "One `<small action>` can `<big result>`" | "One transcript pass removes four of the nine jobs" |
| **less beats more** | Counterintuitive and simplifying at once — "do less" is a relief, not a demand. | "`<fewer X>` beats `<more X>`" | "Fewer edits beat better microphones" |
| **myth-busting** | People enjoy being in the know, and a named lie is more specific than a claim. | "The biggest lie about `<topic>` is `<action>`" | "The biggest lie about podcast audio is that it starts with the mic" |
| **everything wrong** | A total reframe, but the **because** is load-bearing — without a reason it is just noise. | "Everything you know about `<topic>` is wrong, because `<reason>`" | "Everything about your edit is backwards, because you are searching by ear" |
| **reset** | Permission to start clean. Removes the sunk cost of what they already learned. | "Forget what you know about `<topic>`" | "Forget everything you learned about audio timelines" |
| **the unexpected** | Pattern recognition across a group reads as insight rather than opinion. | "The one thing every `<group>` does" | "The one thing every show that publishes weekly has in common" |
| **number-led** | A figure feels objective. Only with a real one — see the rule below. | "`<N>` `<things>` that `<outcome>`" | "6 stages between your recording and a published episode" |
| **admission** | The only move that makes the brand fallible, and a reader who believes you were wrong will believe the rest. | "`<the loss>`, and `<what caused it>`" | "The episode we never shipped, and the four hours that killed it" |
| **future shock** | A change already arriving creates urgency without a deadline. | "`<X>` is about to `<become/stop>`" | "Waveform editing is becoming a specialism, not a skill" |

### Two rules that come with them

**Create a curiosity gap: promise the value, withhold the detail.** A hook that contains
its own answer has nothing left to swipe for. "Transcript editing is faster" is closed.
"Everything about your edit is backwards, because you are searching by ear" opens a door
and makes you walk through it.

**Prefer a specific number to a vague quantity — where a real one exists.** "Nine passes"
beats "several passes". This does not contradict the rule against inventing counts: use
the numbers you have, never manufacture the ones you do not. A count you had to invent is
a promise the deck cannot pay.

**Moves combine.** loss × number-led is the strongest pairing available here: "You're
losing an evening a week to nine passes over the same hour" is both.

### Choosing a move

| the goal | reach for |
|---|---|
| they do not know they have a problem | **loss**, **the unexpected** |
| they know, and have accepted it | **tiny change**, **less beats more** |
| they believe something false | **myth-busting**, **everything wrong**, **reset** |
| they have tried and been burned | **admission** |
| they think there is time | **future shock** |
| they want the shape of the answer | **number-led** |

### Moves deliberately NOT adopted, and why

Most published hook libraries are written for personal brands, and their strongest
patterns assume a first-person narrator with a biography. (cast) is a tool. These do not
convert and should not be retried:

- **"I / How I" anything** — "I analysed 100 X", "Steal my process", "I stayed up until
  2 AM testing". A product account speaking as a person is a fiction the reader can feel.
  The brand-safe version of the same energy is **admission**, above, in the first person
  *plural*.
- **Authority steal** — "Netflix's own team ignores…", "Alex Hormozi uses this". A
  positioning and legal hazard from a product account, and it hands a competitor the
  impression.
- **Achievement with constraint** — "How I built X in 3 months while at uni". Requires a
  protagonist.
- **Analysis-based and data-driven** — "I analysed 1,000 posts, here are the top 5". These
  are excellent moves and unavailable to us honestly: there is no dataset, and
  `voice.avoid` plus the no-invented-figures rule closes the shortcut. Revisit if real
  product telemetry ever becomes publishable.

### One tension worth knowing about

Published pattern-fit tables split by platform — TikTok rewards loss and myth-busting,
Instagram rewards tiny-change and resource lists. **We cannot act on that**: one post
renders to both formats from one set of words, by design (see the format axis). Optimising
the hook per platform would mean two posts, which is the thing the cross-post fix exists to
prevent. Write for the harder room — TikTok, where the scroll is faster.

## Word choice — the verb carries more than the pattern

A hook can be the right pattern, the right length and still land flat, because the verb is
doing no work. This is the cheapest improvement available:

> "You're losing time to editing"  →  "You're **bleeding an evening a week** to editing"

Same pattern, same length class. The second one hurts. **A visceral verb attached to a
concrete number is worth more than any structural change** — and both halves are required:
"bleeding time" is vague, "losing 9 hours" is flat, "bleeding 9 hours" is the hook.

**→ The full word banks, the move-to-class matching table and the worked examples are in
`references/trigger_words.md`.** Four classes: **helper** (loss — the strongest for this
product), **thinker** (contrarian), **insider** (use only with a real specific behind it),
and **amplifiers** (one per hook, and a real number beats any of them).

### The rules that matter more than the banks

- **One or two per hook, never more.** Three reads as parody.
- **At most ONE amplifier.** "literally bleeding 15 hours every single week" is three, and
  it is a caricature of a hook rather than a hook.
- **Never a trigger word without a specific.** "hidden truth" is nothing; "the pass nobody
  mentions" is something. The word points at a fact — if there is no fact, drop the word.
- **Do not mix incompatible classes.** "secretly bleeding" is two metaphors fighting.
- **Watch the character budget.** These words are long — "hemorrhaging" spends 12 of about
  45 characters. A strong verb that costs a line is not strong.

### What actually constrains the register — and what does not

**`voice.avoid` is about CLAIMS, not tone.** Read it: "ad-safe", "brand-safe",
"copyright-proof", "guaranteed zero copyright claims". Every entry is a factual assertion
with legal exposure. Not one of them is about being loud. You can write as hard as you
like — the ban is on saying things that are not true, not on saying true things with
force. Those are independent axes and conflating them produces exactly the timid corporate
copy nobody reads.

**The audience settles the rest.** `brief/product.json` describes independent podcasters
and small teams doing their own post — comedy, interview, commentary — who "have something
worth saying; audio production is necessary but not the goal." That is a creator audience.
A press-release register is wrong for them on the merits, not just aesthetically.

So: **write with force.** The limits below are craft limits, not tone limits, and they
would apply to any voice:

- **One or two trigger words, at most one amplifier.** Not because intensity is unbrandly
  — because three stacked amplifiers read as parody in any register. "literally bleeding
  15 hours every single week" is a caricature of a hook.
- **Every trigger word must point at a specific.** "hidden truth" is hype; "the pass
  nobody mentions" is a hook. The failure mode of a loud voice is vagueness, not volume.
- **The character budget is real.** "hemorrhaging" spends 12 of about 45 characters. Cut
  for the line count, never for the structure.

> **Gap worth closing:** the brief has no tone section at all — only claim safety. Until it
> does, register is a judgement made per post rather than a rule you can check, and that
> is why this section describes constraints rather than a voice.

> **On the "10x engagement" figures.** The source these word banks came from attaches
> multipliers — "~10x shares", "~12x engagement". They are not reproduced here, and the
> reason is narrow: the document gives no sample, method or source, so the *magnitudes*
> cannot be cited as fact by anything downstream. That is a statement about what is
> checkable, not a claim that someone made them up.
>
> The mechanism underneath them is well-founded and does not need the numbers. Loss
> aversion is one of the most replicated findings in behavioural economics, and concrete
> beating vague is the oldest rule in copy. Use the word classes with confidence; just do
> not quote a multiplier at anyone, and never let one into a slide — `scoring.md` flags
> every figure in copy for sourcing precisely so that cannot happen by accident.

## Count the characters — this is a rule, not a gauge

**Write the character count next to every candidate.** The single most damaging failure in
this file's history was not a bad hook: it was a good hook that overflowed, got shortened
for layout, and lost its structure on the way down. A counted limit catches that before it
starts.

At `--t-hero` 144px on `statement`, roughly 11 characters a line:

| chars | lines | verdict |
|---|---|---|
| ≤ 45 | 4 | comfortable |
| 46–55 | 5 | fine — the hook is the only thing on the slide |
| 56–65 | 5–6 | tight; tighten the outcome, not the shape |
| > 65 | 6+ | swamps the art and reads as a wall. Rewrite, do not trim. |

## Use the kicker as the first half of a contrast

`statement` renders the kicker in parentheses above the headline. Today it is usually a
label — "( For anyone who edits their own )" — which spends a whole line saying nothing.

It can carry the belief that the headline then inverts, which is the two-line hook shape
that skill is built around:

```
( Everyone says buy a better mic )
Your problem was never the *microphone*
```

Not every hook wants this — a plain label is fine when the headline is already a complete
promise — but a wasted kicker on a weak headline is two lines doing one line's work.

## Process: write six, then present them as a filled table

Do not write one hook and refine it. Write **six**, across different moves, and hand them
over as a table the user can scan. Six is enough to cover an outcome-promise, a
contrarian, a loss and an admission without padding; fewer and you are defending the first
thing you thought of.

### The table IS the check

Every candidate gets a filled row. This is not a formatting preference — **a column you
have to fill is a step you cannot skip**, and each of these columns exists because it was
once skipped and the miss shipped:

| # | move | hook | chars | lines | trigger word · class | what the deck must pay |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

- **chars / lines** — the hook that overflowed and got shortened for layout lost its
  structure on the way down. Counting before, not after, is what prevents that.
- **trigger word · class** — the verb is where a hook is won, and it is the column most
  often left empty: hooks written from the moves and the templates come out structurally
  correct and verbally flat. Name the word AND which bank it came from
  (`trigger_words.md`) — naming the bank is what stops "impactful" being passed off as a
  trigger word.
- **what the deck must pay** — a hook is a promise. Write the obligation down beside it,
  because that is when you notice you cannot keep it.

**A blank cell means the hook is not finished.** The one legitimate blank is the trigger
word on a structural promise — "Here is how you can actually…" resists one, and the rules
say do not force it. Write `— structural` rather than leaving it empty, so the reader can
tell a decision from an omission.

### Then say what is wrong with them

Alongside the table, flag every candidate that carries a problem the user cannot see:

- **A promise you cannot pay.** "How long your edit actually takes" obliges a duration.
  If there is no sourceable figure, say so — that exact hook killed a previous post,
  which asked the question and never answered it.
- **A choice that is not yours.** An admission hook makes the brand fallible in the first
  person plural. That is a positioning decision, not a wording one.

**Never hedge.** A weak hook is worse than no hook — it spends the only attention the post
gets. If none of the six is strong, the problem is the topic, not the wording; go back and
ask what actually hurts the reader.

## Traps

Every one of these was written in this repo by someone who had just read the templates.

| trap | wrong | why it fails |
|---|---|---|
| **Command, not promise** | "Edit your whole episode without a waveform" | The templates promise INFORMATION. An imperative orders the reader around and gives them no reason to swipe. |
| **Method, not outcome** | "…without a waveform" — a technique | The originals name the END STATE: dream body, goal body, see results. Nobody wants "editing without a waveform"; they want their evening back. |
| **`without` on the wrong noun** | `<method> without <tool>` | The shape is `<outcome> without <the part they dread>`. The dread is the lost hours, not the waveform widget. |
| **Product angle dressed as a topic** | "Music is generative, not stock" | True, and an answer to a question the reader has not asked. Start from what hurts; the feature arrives later as relief rather than as a claim. |
| **Literary register** | "Why one hour of tape eats a whole evening" | No second person, no deliverable in the first six words. It sounds like a headline instead of an offer. |

### The trap that produces most of the others: shortening for layout

A hook that overflows its slide has to get shorter — but shorten the **words**, never the
structure. One version here started at 71 characters and six lines, got cut to 44, and lost
the promise, the outcome and the template in a single edit. If the template will not fit,
find a shorter *outcome*; do not switch to a different kind of sentence.

Rough gauge for `statement` at `--t-hero` 144px: about 11 characters a line, so

- ~45 characters → 4 lines
- ~55 characters → 5 lines, which a hook slide can carry, since it is the only thing on it
- ~70 characters → 6 lines, which swamps the art and reads as a wall

## Turning a product angle into a hook

The mechanical version of "start from the pain": take what the product does, ask what it
costs the reader when they do not have it, and promise the absence of that cost.

| Product angle (wrong hook) | The pain underneath (right hook) |
|---|---|
| "Music is generative, not stock" | "5 ways to score an episode without auditioning stock tracks" |
| "We're not a video editor" | "Here is how you can actually edit audio without opening a video timeline" |
| "Transcript-based editing" | "How long your podcast edit actually takes" |
| "Automatic censoring" | "5 ways to clean up an explicit episode without hunting the waveform" |
| "Auto-ducking and loudness presets" | "Here is how you know if your episode is too quiet to publish" |
